# Formal and informal

German draws a clear line between formal and informal, and it runs through the whole language. The most visible sign is that there are two words for *you*: *du* for the informal and *Sie* for the formal. This is not only grammar. It is a real social signal, and Germans notice it. Before you learn a single greeting, it helps to know that this line exists and that it is taken seriously.

## When to use it

**Formal:**

- Meeting an adult you do not know
- Speaking to officials, shop staff, doctors, or older neighbours
- Any workplace or business setting

**Informal:**

- Talking with friends and family
- Speaking to children
- Fellow students, and usually people your own age in relaxed settings

## Key phrases

| German | English | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| *du* | you (informal) | friends, family, children |
| *Sie* | you (formal) | strangers, formal settings; always capitalised |
| *Herr* / *Frau* | Mr / Mrs | formal address with the surname |
| Wie heißt du? / Wie heißen Sie? | What is your name? | informal / formal |
| Wie geht es dir? / Wie geht es Ihnen? | How are you? | informal / formal |

## Two ways to say "you"

The same question changes shape depending on which *you* you choose. The meaning is identical; only the level of formality differs. In formal settings people also use *Herr* or *Frau* with a surname, while informal settings use first names.

**Examples:**

:::{div} ol-examples
Woher kommst du?
: Where are you from? (informal)

Woher kommen Sie?
: Where are you from? (formal)

Hallo Anna, wie geht es dir?
: Hello Anna, how are you? (informal, first name)

Guten Tag, Frau Weber, wie geht es Ihnen?
: Good day, Mrs Weber, how are you? (formal, surname)
:::

## Why it matters

Using *du* with the wrong person can feel too familiar, almost like a stranger patting you on the shoulder. Using *Sie* with a close friend sounds cold and distant. Germans read these choices as a sign of respect and social awareness, which is why the safe default with anyone new is *Sie*. You wait for the other person to offer *du*, or you offer it yourself once you know them.

:::{tip}
Do not let this scare you. At this stage any competent German speaker can tell at once that you are learning, and that earns you a lot of forgiveness. Mix up *du* and *Sie* and people will simply understand. Keep it in mind as you go, though, because the more fluent and confident you sound, the more a native speaker will expect you to get it right.
:::

## Summary

:::{admonition} Key points
:class: note

- German has two words for *you*: *du* (informal) and *Sie* (formal).
- The choice is a social signal, not just grammar, and Germans take it seriously.
- With anyone new, default to *Sie* until *du* is offered.
- As a beginner you get plenty of forgiveness, so stay aware but do not be afraid to make mistakes.
:::

:::{admonition} Common mistakes
:class: caution

- Using *du* with an adult stranger → start with *Sie* until *du* is offered. (too familiar otherwise)
- Writing *sie* for the formal *you* → **Sie** is always capitalised. (lowercase *sie* means she or they)
:::
